# Task 1 discovery guide — ready to run

This is the thing the case actually grades hardest, and it's the one part
of this submission nobody can do for you. Budget a weekend. Split 20
interviews across the team (4-5 people × 4-5 interviews each is very
doable in 2 days).

## Screening quotas

Aim for spread, not a random 20:

| Dimension | Target split |
|---|---|
| Generation | ~8 Gen Z (18-27) · ~7 Millennial (28-42) · ~5 Gen X/Boomer (43+) — the case wants "at least two generations"; three is stronger evidence |
| City tier | ~12 Tier 1 (metro) · ~8 Tier 2/3 — fine to lean on wherever your team actually has access, but don't submit 20 metro interviews and call it done |
| Household role | Mix of primary cook, occasional cook, and "orders in / doesn't cook" — the non-cooks are as informative as the cooks |
| Life stage | At least a few multi-generational households (parent + adult child under one roof) — the case flags multigenerational influence explicitly |

## Before you knock

- Go in pairs if you can: one person talks, one takes notes verbatim
  (exact words, not paraphrases — paraphrasing is where insight dies).
- Ask permission to open the fridge and the condiment shelf *before* you
  ask anything else. It's a better icebreaker than small talk, and what's
  actually in there matters more than what they say is in there.
- Don't lead with "would you buy a Kissan chutney." Get to that last, if
  at all. Leading with the product kills the discovery.

## The interview, in order

**1. Warm-up (5 min).** "Walk me through what you ate yesterday — every
meal, every snack." Just listen. Note anything dipped, spread, or
condiment-adjacent without prompting it.

**2. The fridge/shelf audit (5 min).** With permission: what condiments
are actually in there right now? Which are open and half-used vs. sealed
and forgotten? Which are homemade vs. bought? Ask "why this one" for
each, and "when did you last use it."

**3. The six case questions, asked as stories not surveys (20-25 min).**
Don't read these as a checklist — use them to steer, and always chase the
follow-up "tell me about a specific time."

1. **What drives experimentation?** "When's the last time you tried a
   food or flavour you'd never had before? What made you try it? What
   made you *not* try the one before that?"
2. **Which food rituals are non-negotiable?** "Is there a dish, a
   condiment, a way of eating something that you'd never change, no
   matter what? Why that one?"
3. **Where are you seeking convenience?** "What's something you used to
   make at home that you now buy? What's something you'd never buy
   ready-made?" (Chutney is very likely to come up unprompted here —
   don't introduce it yourself; let them.)
4. **What flavours or experiences feel missing today?** "If you could
   order/buy one food thing that doesn't exist yet, what would it be?"
5. **How do you discover new food brands, and what gets you to actually
   try one?** "Tell me about the last new food product you bought for
   the first time. Where did you first hear about it? How long between
   hearing about it and buying it?"
6. **What makes a brand feel 'new/exciting' vs. 'old/safe'?** Show them
   two or three condiment brands (mix legacy and challenger, don't
   announce which is which) and ask them to sort into "feels new to me"
   vs "feels like my parents' brand" — then ask *why*, specifically:
   is it the pack, the colour, the name, where they saw it, who else
   uses it?

**4. The condiment-specific probe (5-10 min), only after the above.**
"How do you feel about bottled/packaged chutney specifically, versus
homemade?" "Would you ever put ketchup on something 'Indian' or chutney
on something 'Western'? Have you already?" "If Kissan — a brand you
already trust for ketchup — made a chutney, what would you expect it to
be like? What would make you trust it, or not?"

**5. Close.** "Anything I should have asked and didn't?" — often the best
line of the whole interview.

## Synthesis template (fill this in after all 20)

For each of the six questions, pull 3-5 **verbatim** quotes (not
summaries) that recur across multiple interviews — recurrence across
people is what makes something an insight instead of an anecdote. Note
which generation/city tier each quote came from.

Then answer, in your own words, grounded in what you actually heard (not
what you expected to hear):

- What's the one sentence a majority of your 20 would nod along to about
  how they eat today?
- Where did the "convenience" answer and the "non-negotiable ritual"
  answer point in the *same* direction? (That intersection is usually
  where the real opportunity is.)
- Did "chutney" come up unprompted before you asked about it? From whom,
  and in what context?
- Did the "new vs. old brand" sort produce a consistent pattern, or was
  it all over the place? What specifically triggered "new" in the ones
  that did sort cleanly?

## How this connects to the hypothesis already in `analysis/analysis.md`

That file proposes a white space and a Big Idea built from secondary
research (market data, competitor strategy) — not from these interviews,
because they haven't happened yet. Treat it as a hypothesis to break, not
a conclusion to confirm. If your 20 interviews point somewhere else,
**follow the interviews** — that's the whole point of Task 1, and a jury
will see through a team that did the research and then ignored it.
